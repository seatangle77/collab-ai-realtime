                                  ABSTRACT

   Engagement is a core factor affecting the effectiveness of face-to-face group collabo-
ration, but existing interventions mostly rely on screen-based interfaces, making it difficult
to provide real-time support without interrupting natural conversation. The private HUD-
based feedback provided by smart glasses offers a new design space for face-to-face col-
laboration. Focusing on behavioral and cognitive engagement, this thesis first identified
the key pain points of imbalanced participation and three design goals through a formative
study, based on which EngageCue 1.0 was developed. The behavioral engagement study (N
= 36, 12 groups, within-subjects) showed that the system significantly improved the balance
of group speaking distribution, while interviews also revealed the boundary of behavioral
nudging. Based on this, EngageCue 2.0 was further developed to generate guidance cues an-
chored in specific utterances through real-time analysis of members’cognitive engagement
states. The cognitive engagement study (N = 72, 24 groups, between-subjects) showed that
the smart glasses condition significantly increased the proportion of higher-order cognitive
engagement and shifted discourse structure from divergent exploration to integrative syn-

thesis, but task performance did not significantly improve. Together, the studies show that
smart-glasses-based AI support can improve collaborative processes and discourse struc-
tures, but its transfer to outcome-level performance is limited; cue presentation and con-
tent quality jointly determine whether the intervention can truly reach users’cognitive and
decision-making processes.

                              INTRODUCTION
Face-to-face group collaboration is a core approach for solving complex problems, advanc-
ing collective decision-making, and constructing knowledge, and is widely used in settings
such as classroom discussions, project meetings, and design reviews. In this process, en-
gagement is widely regarded as a core variable that determines collaboration quality and
outcomes. Balanced and in-depth participation is associated with greater discussion sat-
isfaction and better information integration [1], and is also an important prerequisite for
effective collaborative performance [2]. At the behavioral level, imbalanced participation
is a long-standing challenge that is difficult to self-correct [3]. Existing interventions, such
as shared displays, audio feedback, and conversational agents, are mostly limited to online
or virtual scenarios [4, 5], making it difficult to provide low-disruption real-time support
in face-to-face collaboration. At the cognitive level, group discussions often remain at the
stage of divergent exploration and are difficult to spontaneously advance to higher-order
cognitive stages such as integration and synthesis [6], while existing scripted interventions
are difficult to adapt to the real-time rhythm of face-to-face interaction [7].

   Behavioral engagement concerns how observable participation behaviors are distributed
across members and influences the range of information and perspectives represented in
group discussion. Cognitive engagement concerns how ideas are elaborated, justified, and
integrated, influencing whether these contributions can develop into shared understanding
and grounds for collective decision-making. Supporting engagement in face-to-face collab-
oration therefore requires attention to both the distribution of participation and the substan-
tive development of discussion content.

   With the development of AI technologies, researchers have begun to introduce AI into
group collaboration support, exploring how intelligent systems can identify members’ par-
ticipation patterns [8], maintain discussion progress and goal alignment [9], generate per-
sonalized guidance cues [5], and, at the level of cognitive engagement, combine generative

                                               1
AI with support for divergent thinking and idea integration [10, 11]. However, existing
AI-supported collaboration tools mostly target online or asynchronous scenarios and rely
on screen-based interfaces and virtual agents for intervention. Their sensing and feedback
mechanisms are difficult to directly transfer to the physical space of face-to-face collabora-
tion, and real-time dynamic engagement support in face-to-face settings remains an under-
explored research direction.

   With their first-person perspective, hands-free interaction, and HUD-style information
overlay, smart glasses can provide users with private real-time feedback without interrupting
natural conversation, significantly reducing visual attention shifts across devices [12, 13].
This feature makes them particularly suitable for collaborative scenarios that require sus-
tained attention to interpersonal interaction. Prior studies have demonstrated their feasi-
bility in scenarios such as classroom feedback, cross-language communication, and team
operation coordination [14, 15, 16]. However, existing work has mostly focused on re-
mote assistance scenarios characterized by information asymmetry. How smart glasses can
dynamically sense members’ states and support real-time engagement regulation in face-to-
face group collaboration remains insufficiently explored.

   Although the two directions above have each accumulated a certain research founda-
tion, there is still a lack of systematic design exploration and empirical research on how
to use smart glasses as a low-disruption medium, combined with AI-based real-time sens-
ing capabilities, to dynamically support members’ behavioral and cognitive engagement in
face-to-face group collaboration. To address this gap, this thesis is guided by the following
four research questions:

RQ1: How should a smart-glasses-based AI system be designed to support behavioral
engagement balance in face-to-face group collaboration?

RQ2: Can the system improve behavioral engagement balance and overall engage-
ment levels in face-to-face group collaboration? How do users perceive and respond
to system cues?

RQ3: Building on behavioral engagement support, how should the system be further
designed and upgraded to support cognitive engagement in face-to-face group discus-

                                              2
sion?

RQ4: Can the upgraded system improve the quality of cognitive engagement in group
discussion? How do users perceive and accept this form of cognitive engagement sup-
port?

   To address the above research questions, this thesis follows a progressive trajectory from
formative exploration to behavioral engagement support and then to cognitive engagement
support, as shown in Figure 1.1. Each stage builds on the findings of the preceding stage and
progressively addresses RQ1–RQ4 through corresponding system development and user
studies.

  design goals (DG1–DG3) through expert interviews, a probe prototype, and co-design

   To answer RQ1, this thesis conducted a formative study that systematically collected
design evidence through three progressive stages: expert interviews, probe prototype de-
velopment, and co-design workshops. The study identified two forms of imbalanced partic-
ipation in face-to-face collaboration—under-expression and over-expression—and derived
three design goals (DG1–DG3). It also confirmed the feasibility of the smart glasses HUD
as a low-disruption private feedback channel, providing an empirical foundation for subse-
quent system design.

   Based on DG1–DG3, this thesis designed and implemented EngageCue 1.0, a smart-
glasses-based support system that integrates multimodal behavioral sensing and AI-generated

                                             3
cues. The system collected members’ speech, browsing, and editing data in parallel through
a shared microphone, browser plugin, and collaborative note-taking interface. It quantified
each member’s participation level using a 2-minute analysis window, and the AI module
generated personalized cues that were presented in real time through the smart glasses HUD
with brief and gentle wording, supporting engagement balance regulation without interrupt-
ing natural face-to-face conversation.

   To answer RQ2, this thesis recruited 36 participants in 12 groups and conducted a user
study using a within-subject design. The results showed that EngageCue 1.0 significantly
improved the balance of group speaking distribution, but overall engagement levels did not
significantly increase. Interview data further revealed that when participation bottlenecks
stemmed from a lack of cognitive content rather than insufficient social motivation, behav-
ioral cues could not address the root cause. This finding defined the boundary of behavioral
nudging and shifted the research focus toward support for cognitive engagement.

   To answer RQ3, this thesis conducted a secondary analysis of the qualitative data from
the Study 1 user study, identifying three types of cognitive challenges: idea stagnation, shal-
low elaboration, and differences in knowledge understanding. It then derived three design
goals for cognitive engagement (DG1! –DG3! ) and developed EngageCue 2.0 accordingly.
Compared with the 1.0 version, EngageCue 2.0 narrowed the sensing modality to speech
as a single channel. By analyzing indicators such as members’ speaking proportion, lex-
ical richness, and argumentation density in real time, the system inferred their cognitive
engagement states and generated corresponding guidance cues when the above cognitive
challenges were detected. These cues were pushed to the smart glasses HUD to help group
discussions move from divergent exploration toward deeper integration.

   To answer RQ4, this thesis conducted a second user study, recruiting 72 participants in
24 groups and adopting a between-subject design. The results showed that the proportion
of higher-order cognitive engagement significantly increased under the smart glasses con-
dition, and the discourse structure showed a measurable shift from divergent exploration to
integrative synthesis. However, final task performance did not significantly improve, and
cases in which cue content was substantively adopted and transformed into decision-making


                                              4
evidence remained limited.

   Together, the two studies reveal a consistent pattern of effects: smart-glasses-based
AI support can produce measurable improvements at the process and structural levels, but
its transfer to outcome-level performance is limited; cues can reshape the distribution and
rhythm of discussion, but they cannot replace members’ own cognitive generation, nor can
they ensure that cue content is truly internalized as a basis for decision-making. The two
studies also show that cue presentation and content quality are equally important, jointly
determining whether an intervention can truly reach users’ cognitive and decision-making
processes.

   The contributions of this thesis are threefold:


   1. This thesis identifies key design goals at both the behavioral and cognitive levels
      of face-to-face group collaboration. Through the Formative Study and a secondary
      analysis of the Study 1 user study, this thesis respectively derived the design goals
      for supporting engagement balance (DG1–DG3) and the design goals for supporting
      cognitive engagement, including strategies for addressing idea stagnation and shallow
      elaboration, providing empirical evidence for the design of smart-glasses-based AI
      support systems.

   2. This thesis designed and implemented two iterative versions of the EngageCue
      system. EngageCue 1.0 supports the balance of behavioral engagement, while En-
      gageCue 2.0 supports cognitive engagement. Both versions use the smart glasses
      HUD as a non-intrusive feedback medium, reflecting the system’s design evolution
      from the behavioral level to the cognitive level.

   3. This thesis provides empirical evidence through two user studies. The user studies
      show that AI support presents a consistent pattern of effects in face-to-face collabo-
      ration: the system can bring measurable improvements at the process and structural
      levels, but its transfer to outcome-level performance is limited. Users’ perceptions
      of cues, including timing, tone, and presentation format, are key moderating factors
      that determine their effects. This finding provides concrete implications for system
      design in this field.

                            RELATED WORK

2.1 Engagement in Face-to-Face Group Collaboration

In face-to-face group collaboration, engagement is widely regarded as a core variable that
determines collaboration quality and outcomes. Engagement is a multidimensional con-
struct that mainly includes three dimensions: behavioral engagement, cognitive engage-
ment, and emotional/social engagement [17]. Among these dimensions, behavioral en-
gagement is reflected in observable behaviors such as speaking and participation frequency,
while cognitive engagement is reflected in cognitive processes such as depth of thinking,
quality of argumentation, and idea integration [17].

   Behavioral engagement is the most direct and easily measurable dimension, and it has
also been a major focus of early related research [2, 18]. A more balanced participation
distribution is usually associated with higher satisfaction and more effective collaborative
performance [1, 19]. In contrast, when a few members dominate the conversation or some
members remain silent for extended periods, groups are more likely to experience informa-
tion neglect, decision bias, and reduced group dynamics [3, 4]. Such imbalanced partici-
pation has been recognized as a long-standing challenge in collaboration that is difficult to
self-correct. Its causes involve status differences and early-formed structures of conversa-
tional dominance [3], as well as social loafing resulting from the difficulty of attributing
individual contributions [20]. To address this issue, prior studies have explored various
technological approaches, including shared displays and interactive tabletops [3, 4], audio
visualization feedback [21], conversational agents [5, 22], and nonverbal cues such as robots
or haptic feedback [23, 24]. However, most of these approaches are limited to online or vir-
tual scenarios, and real-time dynamic participation regulation in face-to-face collaboration
remains an open issue.


                                             8
   Compared with participation frequency at the behavioral level, cognitive engagement
focuses more on the quality of group discussion content itself, including whether members
can propose new ideas, elaborate on and justify existing ideas, and integrate different view-
points into consensual conclusions [25]. The CoI framework divides cognitive engagement
into four phases: Triggering Event, Exploration, Integration, and Resolution [6], suggesting
that group discussions often remain at the level of divergent exploration and have difficulty
spontaneously progressing toward integration, synthesis, and problem solving. However,
members in group discussions often struggle to advance spontaneously to higher-order cog-
nitive stages, and deep argumentation and knowledge construction often require external
structured support [2]. Collaborative scripts are a common form of intervention [7], but they
are difficult to adapt to the real-time rhythm of natural face-to-face conversation. Although
structured task design can guide cognitive processes [26], it is also difficult to provide im-
mediate feedback based on individual states. In recent years, some studies have attempted to
identify cognitive engagement patterns in collaboration [8], automatically detect participa-
tion states in face-to-face settings [27], or provide group feedback based on analytic results
[28]. However, how to transform real-time cognitive engagement recognition into effective
intervention in face-to-face collaboration, thereby helping groups improve the depth and
quality of discussion, remains insufficiently explored through targeted system design and
empirical evaluation.

   Although prior research has explored both behavioral and cognitive engagement, how
to provide real-time dynamic support for both in face-to-face collaboration still lacks sys-
tematic research and design. This constitutes the core starting point of this thesis.



2.2     AI-Supported Group Engagement Tools

With the development of AI technologies, researchers have begun to introduce AI into group
collaboration support, exploring how intelligent systems can improve collaboration quality
and participation outcomes [29, 30]. In terms of behavioral engagement regulation, AI
has been used to identify members’collaborative states and participation patterns in real
time [8], and to provide guidance cues and immediate feedback to group members through

                                              9
conversational agents, so as to maintain discussion progress and goal alignment [5, 31, 9,
32]. In terms of cognitive engagement support, the combination of generative AI and shared
displays has provided new possibilities for supporting group divergent thinking and idea
integration [10, 11]; tiered scaffolding and follow-up questioning cues have also been used
to help members deepen their argumentation and break through fixed patterns of thinking
[33]. However, studies have also found that an excessive level of AI intervention may
instead weaken members’own cognitive engagement [34, 35]. In group decision-making,
embodied agents have been found to help break the dominance of majority opinions and
improve discussion quality [36, 33], but their effects remain influenced by task types and
interaction forms [37].

   Although existing AI-supported collaboration tools mostly target online or asynchronous
scenarios, or rely on screen-based interfaces and virtual agents for intervention, real-time
dynamic engagement support in face-to-face collaboration remains an underexplored re-
search direction and is also one of the entry points of this thesis.



2.3 Smart Glasses as a Collaboration Medium

Smart glasses overlay virtual information onto the physical environment, providing a low-
disruption embodied interaction approach for collaborative scenarios. Their first-person
perspective and hands-free characteristics [12, 38] allow information to be presented with-
out interrupting the current task. Compared with traditional screen-based devices, they sig-
nificantly reduce visual attention shifts across devices [39, 14], helping maintain cognitive
continuity in dynamic contexts [40, 15]. HUD-style, spatially aligned information overlay
can transform abstract content into intuitive environmental cues and reduce cognitive load
[13, 41], making smart glasses particularly suitable for collaborative scenarios that require
sustained attention to interpersonal interaction. In existing applications, smart glasses have
been shown to improve the continuity of information flow in classroom feedback [14] and
cross-language communication [15], and to effectively support team coordination in sce-
narios such as telemedicine [42], engineering operations [16], and medical training [43].

   In contrast, repeatedly checking mobile screens may cause distraction and social pres-

                                              10
sure [44]. Visible device use during face-to-face interaction may also raise privacy and
social acceptability concerns [45], while publicly displaying individual participation states
on shared screens may make group members uncomfortable [46]. A personal HUD on smart
glasses can instead deliver cues only to the intended member, thereby reducing the public
exposure of individual participation states and the associated evaluative pressure. To realize
this advantage, the position and alignment of cues need to be carefully designed for the in-
teraction context to support sustained attention, low intrusiveness, and social acceptability
[47].

    However, existing studies have mostly focused on information-asymmetric scenarios
such as remote assistance or simulation training, with task execution efficiency as the pri-
mary concern. How smart glasses can dynamically sense members’states and support
real-time engagement regulation in face-to-face group collaboration remains insufficiently
explored. This is the core rationale for selecting smart glasses as the intervention medium
for AI support in this thesis.

    Overall, prior research has accumulated a foundation in both engagement support tools
and smart glasses applications. However, in the specific context of face-to-face group col-
laboration, there remains a lack of targeted system design and empirical evidence on how
to translate AI-based real-time sensing and analysis capabilities into effective support for
behavioral and cognitive engagement through a low-disruption wearable device. This is the
core issue that this thesis seeks to address.

3.3 Findings and Design Goals

Through open coding and multiple rounds of thematic synthesis of the recordings, tran-
scripts, whiteboard materials, and sticky notes from the co-design stage, this thesis identified
three key

   findings and derived three design goals accordingly (DG1–DG3).

   DG1: Identify and prompt members who under-express or over-express themselves
to promote balanced verbal participation. Participants reported two forms of participa-
tion imbalance: under-expression, such as remaining silent or relying on notes to express
ideas, and over-expression, such as continuously dominating the conversation and making
it difficult for others to join in. Both forms were considered to affect collaboration quality,
but they required opposite directions of regulation and therefore called for differentiated
cueing mechanisms. In terms of under-expression, some participants (N = 3) reported that
they were reluctant to speak because they were unfamiliar with their teammates: “I was
not very familiar with my teammates, so it felt a bit awkward to speak”(P6, G2). In terms
of over-expression, some participants (N = 4) observed that certain members dominated the
conversation for extended periods: “Some people kept talking, and I felt it was hard to join
in”(P10, G4).

   DG2: Provide flexible and adjustable cue frequency to balance feedback timeliness
with the natural rhythm of discussion. Some participants (N = 4) reported that fixed-
frequency cues could easily become disruptive and reduce their trust in the system: “I
want to control it myself —the cues are too frequent now”(P2, G1). This suggests that the
cueing mechanism needs to balance timely feedback with the rhythm of discussion while
preserving users’autonomy in adjusting the system.

   DG3: Keep cue content concise and friendly to fit the display characteristics of


                                              16
smart glasses and improve user acceptance. Due to the monochrome green waveguide
display and limited field of view of the smart glasses, lengthy text would significantly in-
crease reading burden. Some participants (N = 5) reported that long text was difficult to
perceive quickly under this display condition, and most participants (N = 8) expressed clear
resistance to imperative wording, preferring shorter cues with a gentler and more encourag-
ing tone: “If the tone were softer, people would be more willing to speak”(P4, G2).

4.1 System Design

Based on the three design goals (DG1–DG3) derived from the Formative Study, this thesis
designed and implemented EngageCue, an engagement balance support system that inte-
grates smart glasses and AI. The system follows a closed-loop architecture of “sensing-
analysis- feedback-interaction.” It continuously collects members’ multimodal behavioral
data, processes them through a two-layer analysis pipeline, and provides real-time cues
to members through the smart glasses HUD, supporting engagement balance regulation in
face-to-face collaboration. An overview of the system architecture is shown in Figure 4.1.




 Figure 4.1: EngageCue system architecture and interaction flow. The system collects
 speech, editing, and browsing data through the sensing layer, generates contextualized
cues through local analysis and AI analysis, and provides real-time feedback to members
  through the smart glasses HUD and web interface, forming a continuously operating
                      “sensing-analysis-feedback-interaction” loop.



4.1.1 System Architecture

The Sensing Layer continuously collects three types of behavioral data: speech, editing,
and browsing. Speech is collected through a shared device, with speaker identification used

                                            18
to distinguish speakers and a 0.6-second silence interval used for segmentation to calculate
duration and frequency. Editing data are recorded through each participant’s personal inter-
face, with editing frequency and word count calculated in 10-second windows. Browsing
data are collected every 10 seconds through a browser plugin, including URLs, interaction
events, and mouse behaviors. All data are unified into a single input stream for subsequent
analysis. The 0.6-second silence interval and 10-second logging window standardize the
granularity of speech segmentation and the collection of editing and browsing logs, respec-
tively. These settings are fixed sensing parameters of the prototype and are held constant
across all participants and experimental conditions.

   The Analysis Layer adopts a two-layer architecture: local analysis and AI analysis. In
the probe stage, relying solely on an LLM to interpret multimodal data produced unsta-
ble outputs, which could undermine user trust. Therefore, EngageCue separates decision-
making from expression: local analysis retains the 2-minute window used in the probe stage
to aggregate members’ recent multimodal behaviors. It normalizes speech, editing, and
browsing behaviors into 0–1 scores, combines them into an overall participation score using
weights of 0.7, 0.15, and 0.15, respectively, and maps the resulting score onto four partic-
ipation levels (none/low/medium/high). These parameters serve as operational settings for
the EngageCue 1.0 prototype and are applied consistently across all participants and experi-
mental conditions. The AI analysis module, based on Gemini-1.5-Flash, is responsible only
for transforming discrete participation levels into concise and gentle natural-language cues,
such as “Perhaps you could invite others to share their thoughts” for dominant speakers and
“We would love to hear your thoughts (ˆ-ˆ)” for silent members.

   The Feedback Layer presents cues immediately through the smart glasses HUD in the
form of brief text, symbols, and audio, ensuring that members can receive feedback without
interrupting their current task. All cues use concise wording, softening expressions, and
emoticons to reduce the mechanical tone and social pressure.




                                             19
4.1.2 Interaction Mechanism

In actual use, participants carried out face-to-face discussions and collaborative tasks while
EngageCue 1.0 continuously collected speech, editing, and browsing behaviors in the back-
ground and updated participation states in 2-minute windows. When a member’s participa-
tion level met an intervention condition and the configured cue interval allowed a new cue,
the system privately presented a brief cue to the target member through the smart glasses
HUD. Members could decide whether to follow the cue and adjust their participation be-
havior, and could also regulate cue frequency through the web interface.

   Corresponding to DG1, the system triggers differentiated cues for “silent members”
and “dominant members.” Local analysis classifies the overall participation score into four
levels. The no-activity and low levels trigger encouraging cues, the high level triggers cues
that remind members to appropriately leave speaking space for others, and the medium level
does not trigger intervention by default. The speaking weight is set to 0.7, while editing and
browsing are each weighted at 0.15. This reflects the central role of verbal interaction in
face-to-face collaboration while also accounting for members’ contributions through note-
taking or information seeking at different stages of collaboration.

   Corresponding to DG2, the system provides an autonomous adjustment function, allow-
ing members to adjust the cue interval within a range of 2–4 minutes through the “more/less”
controls on the web interface. This accommodates individual differences in preferred cue
frequency and avoids excessive cues disrupting the rhythm of collaboration.

   Corresponding to DG3, all cues use concise wording and softening expressions, such
as “perhaps,” together with emoticons such as ˆ-ˆ, to reduce the mechanical tone of AI-
generated cues, decrease the social pressure experienced when receiving cues, and improve
users’ willingness to accept them.

4.2.3 Data Collection and Analysis

This thesis adopted a multi-source data collection approach, integrating objective behavioral
data and subjective self-report data to comprehensively evaluate the effects of EngageCue
1.0.

   For objective data, participation balance was measured using the Gini coefficient. The
distribution balance of three types of behavior—speaking, editing, and browsing—was cal-
culated separately, with a lower Gini coefficient indicating more balanced participation.
Group participation level was reflected through two types of indicators: behavioral logs
and message volume. Behavioral logs recorded each member’s speaking duration, speak-
ing frequency, editing activities, and browsing activities. Message volume was calculated
as the total number of text tokens in group members’speech. In addition, the entire experi-
ment was recorded through two video angles, front-facing and overhead. The research team
manually annotated posture, including leaning forward, neutral, and leaning backward, and
gaze direction, including looking at the screen, looking at others, and wandering. These
annotations were mapped into high, medium, and low levels of engagement as multimodal
triangulation for the behavioral logs.

   For subjective data, participants completed the QCGE-SAS scale [48, 49], which covers
four dimensions: Behavioral Engagement (BE), Social Engagement (SE), Cognitive En-
gagement (CE), and Concept-to-Conclusion Engagement (CC), reflecting their subjective
perceptions of collaborative engagement quality. In addition, the research team conducted
approximately 30-minute semi-structured interviews with each group, inviting participants
to compare their collaboration experiences under the two conditions and reflect on their
perceptions of the cueing mechanism and behavioral regulation process.

   For the analysis methods, all quantitative data were compared between conditions us-
ing paired-samples t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, and the corresponding effect sizes
were reported (Cohen’s d for t-tests and r for Wilcoxon signed-rank tests). Interview data
were analyzed using thematic coding, with a codebook developed collaboratively by mul-
tiple researchers, and representative quotes were selected to illustrate differences in user
experience.

 told me that I was doing well”(P16, G6).

    At the level of emotional response, a considerable number of participants (N = 17) re-
 ported clear emotional reactions, including feeling encouraged, constrained, or pressured,
 indicating that the cues triggered affective processing rather than being perceived merely
 as informational reminders. Some participants held positive feelings toward the cues (N =
 8), believing that they provided emotional support. Other participants (N = 8), however,
 interpreted the same tone as perfunctory, mechanical, or even somewhat sarcastic: “I al-
 ready felt that the AI cues on the glasses were a bit mechanical, so its words could not be as
 sincere as those from a real person”(P25, G9).

    At the level of behavioral regulation, more participants (N = 14) explicitly stated that
 the cues prompted them to adjust their participation behaviors. Among them, some partic-
 ipants (N = 9) spoke more actively after receiving cues: “Seeing the cue made me want
 to speak”(P8, G3). A small number of participants (N = 3) exercised self-restraint and
 actively reduced their speaking: “The cue on the glasses made me realize that I might have
 been speaking too much”(P5, G2). In addition, some participants (N = 4) also observed

                                              28
the influence of cues on other members: “I noticed that every time my teammates received
cues on the glasses, they would speak a little more”(P7, G3).

   In terms of cue timing and tone, when cues appeared during an ongoing task flow or
thinking process, most participants (N = 16) perceived them as disruptive. Participants who
considered the timing appropriate (N = 10) mainly did so because the cues appeared during
pauses in the discussion. Although the system adopted gentle and encouraging wording to-
gether with emoticons, participants’perceptions of its sincerity were clearly divided. Some

which to some extent constrained the emotional effect of the cues.

      STUDY 2: COGNITIVE ENGAGEMENT

Study 1 showed that behavioral-level nudging could improve speaking balance, but the in-
terview data also revealed its boundary: when participation bottlenecks stemmed from a lack
of cognitive content rather than insufficient social motivation, behavioral cues were unlikely
to produce substantive change. This boundary shifted the research focus to a deeper ques-
tion: how to support the deepening of group discussion at the cognitive level. Based on this,
Study 2 further focused on speech interaction and language content. Speech interaction is
not only the most direct and explicit form of participation in face-to-face collaboration, re-
flecting the speaking distribution and interaction rhythm among group members [50], but is
also the main medium through which cognitive engagement unfolds: members need to use
language to raise questions, express viewpoints, integrate others’opinions, and advance
shared understanding [51]. At the same time, knowledge construction and argumentation
deepening in collaboration are also mainly reflected in how members use discourse to pro-
vide reasons, respond to counterarguments, and connect different viewpoints [52]. There-
fore, the system goal of Study 2 further advanced from behavioral engagement regulation
to cognitive engagement support.



5.1 Design Goals for Cognitive Engagement

To clarify the specific design directions for cognitive engagement support, this thesis con-
ducted a secondary analysis of the qualitative data from the Study 1 user study. Through
open coding and thematic synthesis, three typical cognitive challenges were identified, and
three design goals were derived accordingly (DG1! –DG3! ).

   DG1! : The system should provide generative topic support, injecting new discus-
sion entry points when individuals or the group fall into idea stagnation, thereby help-
ing restart or sustain the discussion. Participants reported two forms of idea stagnation:

                                             30
individual cold-start difficulties and the whole group becoming stuck at the same time. Some
participants (N = 13) clearly expressed a need for external topic support: “There was a pe-
riod when we got stuck for dozens of seconds, and everyone had run out of ideas. Actually,
AI could guide us at that moment”(P11, G3). “At the beginning, I was a bit stuck... I did
not have many ideas on this abstract topic”(P2, G1).

   DG2! : When the system detects that a member’s speech lacks argumentative struc-
ture or supporting evidence, it should push follow-up guiding questions to that mem-
ber, helping them move from “participation in quantity”to “deepening in quality.”
Some participants (N = 8) reported that the discussion remained at the level of superficial
opinions and expressed dissatisfaction that the system only focused on participation fre-
quency while neglecting speech quality: “Do not just urge me; it could guide my thinking
more”(P22, G7).“This AI did not analyze the content of what we said... It would be better
to add cognitive guidance”(P10, G3).

   DG3! : The system should provide on-demand knowledge supplementation support,
helping members bridge differences in understanding through concept explanations
and information supplementation. Some participants (N = 6) experienced understanding
barriers due to knowledge gaps or conceptual disagreements, and tended to actively seek
information when needed: “I was not clear about some of the terms they mentioned... It
would be very helpful if AI could help me list these terms and explain them”(P6, G1).

Based on the three design goals (DG1! –DG3! ) derived from the secondary analysis of the
qualitative data from the Study 1 user study, this thesis designed and implemented Engage-
Cue 2.0—a smart-glasses-based support system that shifts from supporting behavioral en-
gagement balance in EngageCue 1.0 to supporting the cognitive deepening of group dis-
cussion. The system continues the overall closed-loop architecture of “sensing–analysis–
feedback.”An overview of the system architecture is shown in Figure 5.1.

5.2.1 System Architecture

Based on the practical feedback from Study 1, EngageCue 2.0 narrowed the sensing modal-
ities at the sensing layer from three types—speech, editing, and browsing—to speech as a
single channel. This narrowing was based on two considerations. First, editing and brows-
ing indicators had relatively weak associations with cognitive engagement states and were
difficult to use to reflect members’depth of thinking about discussion content. Second, both
indicators depended heavily on devices; once members shifted to purely oral discussion,
these indicators became distorted. In the Study 1 interviews, some members had already
reported that such misjudgments caused unnecessary disruption. Correspondingly, the col-
laborative task was simplified from a computer-dependent format to a paper-and-pencil task,
aligning sensing capability with task design. After speech was collected, it was transformed
into structured discussion transcripts, which served as the only input to the analysis layer.

   The analysis layer consisted of two levels: perception analysis and action decision-
making. Perception analysis ran periodically at a fixed frequency and calculated a set of

on an utterance-by-utterance basis [33]. Among these indicators, semantic repetition was
used to measure the degree of content overlap among different utterances by a member

by examining the distributions of the indicators and the corresponding trigger cases. This
calibration aimed to identify clear cases of insufficient participation or shallow elaboration
while reducing excessive triggering. The Study 1 data were used only for parameter cali-
bration, whereas the system effects were evaluated using independently collected Study 2
data. These thresholds would require recalibration for other tasks, group sizes, or participant
populations.

   In the member-level analysis, the AI operated in two consecutive stages. First, the
perception analysis model received recent utterances grouped by member and determined
whether each member’s contributions contained an explicit reasoning structure and support-
ing evidence. It returned the reasoning status, evidence status, and corresponding judgment
rationales in structured JSON. The system then combined these results with the current dis-
cussion transcript, the staged summary, and member-level indicators, including speaking

                                              34
proportion, silence duration, lexical richness, semantic repetition, and argumentation den-
sity, and passed the combined structured input to the action decision-making model. The
model classified each member as experiencing idea stagnation, shallow elaboration, or re-
quiring no intervention [9, 22]. When intervention was warranted, the model also returned
a concise cue and the specific utterance to which the cue was anchored [33]. When the
available evidence was insufficient or a reliable judgment could not be made, the system
defaulted to no intervention to reduce false triggers and unnecessary disruption.

   The feedback layer delivered the intervention text produced by the action decision-
making layer to the target member through a process of “queueing first, filtering next,
and presenting last.”The text sequentially passed through four layers of filtering: conflict
removal, duplicate filtering, semantic similarity interception, and VAD-based speech detec-
tion, ensuring that cues were presented during natural pauses in the discussion. Member-
level interventions were pushed only to the target member, such as the follow-up cue Amy
received in Figure 5.1: “Is whiskey more reliable than matches for keeping warm?”Group
silence cues were broadcast directly to the whole group and then entered a cooldown period,
continuing the consistent feedback from users in the Formative Study and Study 1 regard-
ing cue frequency and timing. The detailed prompt design for cue generation is provided in
Appendix A.7.


5.2.2    Interaction Mechanism

Corresponding to DG1! , the system triggers differentiated interventions for two forms of
idea stagnation: individual stagnation and group-level stagnation. At the individual level,
when a member’s speaking proportion remains extremely low or their silence duration
exceeds the threshold, the system extracts specific points of contention from the current
discussion and generates a targeted follow-up question to push to that member, helping them
re-enter the discussion. At the group level, when the whole group remains silent beyond the
threshold, the system generates an ice-breaking topic and broadcasts it to all members. After
being triggered, the system enters a cooldown period to avoid repeatedly interrupting the
discussion rhythm.


                                             35
   Corresponding to DG2! , the system uses whether an utterance contains a clear argumen-
tative structure and supporting evidence as the core judgment criterion. When a member has
a reasonable amount of speech but completely lacks reasons or factual evidence, an inter-
vention is triggered. If only weaker signals, such as low argumentation density, are present,
multiple signals must be combined before triggering, so as to avoid over-intervening in con-
cise speaking styles. The intervention text is anchored to the member’s specific utterance
and asks follow-up questions about their unelaborated implicit stance or missing evidence.

   Corresponding to DG3! , the system continuously identifies keywords in the discussion
that may involve barriers to understanding and evaluates semantic differences in members’
understanding of these terms. When a potential information gap is detected, the system
pushes a lightweight prompt button to the target member. The member can actively click it
to generate an immediate explanation, thereby supporting on-demand information access in
response to users’preference for autonomy in information supplementation.


5.2.3    System Implementation

EngageCue 2.0 retained the hardware setup used in Study 1 and was implemented using
the StarV Air2 smart glasses and the companion mobile application. Speech was collected
through a shared microphone, transcribed using Tencent real-time ASR, and combined with
speaker identification to distinguish speakers, generating structured discussion transcripts.
The system aggregated discussion transcripts every minute and ran perception analysis and
action decision-making every two minutes.

   AI analysis used the Qwen series models: larger models handled perception analy-
sis and comprehensive member-level decision-making, while lightweight models handled
low-latency tasks such as ice-breaking topic generation. The analysis stages exchanged re-
sults through structured JSON. Text similarity was calculated using the locally deployed
paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2 model. Generated cues passed through conflict
removal, duplicate filtering, semantic similarity filtering, and VAD-based speech detection,
and group-level cues entered a cooldown period after delivery to reduce repetitive or mist-
imed interventions. A self-deployed PostgreSQL relational database managed session met-


                                             36
rics, discussion transcripts, and intervention records, while the Vue frontend supported ac-
count, group, and session management as well as interaction with information-gap prompts.
The full prompts and JSON schemas are provided in Appendix A.7.



5.3     User Study

5.3.1    Participants

This thesis recruited 72 participants from a university community (P1–P72, Mage = 23.8
years, SD = 2.9). Before the experiment, they were randomly assigned to 24 groups, with
three participants in each group. Participants included undergraduate students, master’s
students, doctoral students, and research assistants, with diverse disciplinary backgrounds.
All participants had experience with face-to-face group discussion. The 24 groups were
randomly assigned to the two experimental conditions, with 12 groups assigned to the no-
assistance condition and 12 groups assigned to the smart glasses condition. The study was
approved by the institutional ethics review board. Participants took part voluntarily, signed
informed consent forms, and could withdraw at any time without any consequences. All par-
ticipants received compensation according to local standards. Detailed group assignments
and demographic information are provided in Appendix Table A.3.


5.3.2    Study Design and Procedure

This thesis adopted a between-subject design. Each group completed one 30-minute face-
to-face group discussion task and was assigned either to the smart glasses condition, the
experimental condition, or to the no-assistance condition, the control condition. Under the
smart glasses condition, the system overlaid AI-generated cognitive cues in real time within
participants’field of view through the StarV Air2 optical waveguide HUD. Under the no-
assistance condition, participants also wore smart glasses, but push notifications were not
enabled, and the phones were kept by the researcher to exclude the influence of the device
itself on participation behavior.

5.3.3 Data Collection and Analysis

This thesis adopted a multi-source data collection approach, integrating objective behavioral
data and subjective self-report data to comprehensively evaluate the effects of EngageCue
2.0.

   For objective data, task performance was assessed using scores from the survival ranking
tasks. Each individual or group ranking was compared item by item with the correspond-
ing expert ranking, and the sum of the absolute rank differences constituted the task score;
lower scores indicated better performance. The individual score (IS) and group score (GS)
represented the pre-discussion individual ranking score and the post-discussion group con-
sensus score, respectively. The average individual score (AIS) was the mean IS of the three
group members, whereas the best IS was the lowest IS within the group. Weak synergy was
calculated as AIS → GS and strong synergy as best IS → GS; positive values indicated that
the group outperformed the corresponding individual baseline, whereas negative values in-
dicated that it did not. These measures captured outcome-level ranking accuracy, while the
cognitive engagement process was assessed through the subsequent CoI coding. Detailed
task descriptions and scoring rules are provided in Table 5.1.

   Cognitive engagement quality was assessed by coding the discussion transcripts using
Cognitive Presence in the CoI framework [51], covering four phases: Triggering Event, Ex-
ploration, Integration, and Resolution. Before coding, the transcripts were checked against
the original audio to correct transcription errors and were organized into viewpoint units us-
ing predefined segmentation and integration rules. Coding decisions were based on the cog-
nitive function of each viewpoint unit within its conversational context. Detailed viewpoint-
unit organization rules and operational definitions of the four CoI categories are provided
in Appendices A.5 and A.6. The proportions of discourse in each phase, the proportion of
higher-order cognitive engagement (Integration + Resolution), and weighted scores were


                                             40
calculated. In addition, ENA (Epistemic Network Analysis) was used as a supplementary
method [55]. Based on a 2-minute sliding window with a 30-second step size, ENA ana-
lyzed the co-occurrence connection strength among different cognitive phases to examine
whether group discussion formed a cognitive progression structure from Exploration to In-
tegration to Resolution.

5.4.3    User Experience Observations

Interview coding further revealed several characteristics of smart glasses support at the ex-
periential level. At the perceptual level, participants in all 12 groups noticed the presence
of cues, but the quality of perception varied. Some participants were able to actively read
the cue content one by one: “I felt that I could scan what it said all at once”(P13, G05).
Other groups formed a negative impression because the first cue was of poor quality, which
led subsequent cues to be systematically ignored: “The first message received was not that
useful, so we would not pay attention to it”(P36, G12).

   At the level of emotional response, positive and negative reactions were roughly bal-
anced across groups, and clear differences often emerged among different members within
the same group. Some participants felt strongly inspired by the cues, while others held a
wholly negative attitude. Notably, some participants showed a separation between subjec-
tive evaluation and actual behavior. Even when they verbally rejected the cues, their actual
discussion was still influenced by them: “I think every one of them was extremely stupid”
(P31, G11), but the interview showed that this participant still accepted an argument from
the cue and adjusted their stance.


                                             45
   At the timing level, four of the 12 groups reported that cues clearly interrupted ongoing
speech or thinking, while three groups reported that the main issue was delay rather than
interruption: “It was a relatively useful suggestion, but actually we had already discussed
this issue at that time... After we finished discussing it, the AI raised the same thing again,
so it was delayed”(P35, G12).

   At the level of cue format, interview coding revealed systematic differences between
interrogative and declarative formats in their effects on cognitive integration. Participants
generally expressed a preference for declarative cues: “It would be better if it just gave
us some evidence-based things and let us judge by ourselves”(P25, G09). Interrogative
cues, by contrast, tended to make participants choose to trust their own judgments rather
than engage in deeper questioning.

   In addition, participants under the no-assistance condition also actively expressed spe-
cific expectations for AI support. They tended to expect AI to provide factual background
knowledge rather than directly provide ranking conclusions: “If AI could provide some
background knowledge, it would help us, but for the specific ranking... everyone would
move toward that answer”(P30, G10). This feedback provides further evidence for the
discussion of cue design in Chapter 6.

   In summary, the user experience results indicate that the presentation format of cues—
including the quality of the first cue, the precision of timing, and the choice of sentence
form—has a key influence on their acceptance. This finding is highly consistent with the
observations in Study 1 and provides further evidence for the discussion of cue design in
Chapter 6.

                                 DISCUSSION

6.1     From Opportunity Balance to Content Deepening: The
        Design Boundary of Cues

The two user studies together present a consistent pattern: smart-glasses-based AI support
can change the processes and structures of face-to-face collaboration, but such changes do
not necessarily translate into improvements at the outcome level. In Study 1, EngageCue
1.0 significantly improved the balance of speaking distribution. In Study 2, EngageCue
2.0 further increased the proportion of higher-order cognitive engagement and shifted the
discourse structure from exploratory divergence toward integrative synthesis. However, the
process-level improvements in both studies did not consistently translate into significant
improvements in overall engagement level or task performance. This indicates that smart-
glasses-based AI support has a hierarchical boundary in face-to-face collaboration: it is
more likely to affect“how collaboration happens”than to directly change“what outcomes
collaboration produces.”

   At the behavioral level, this boundary is reflected in the following: behavioral cues
can redistribute “who speaks,”but they are less able to address “what to say.”Study 1
showed that private HUD cues could help silent members gain more opportunities to ex-
press themselves and could also remind dominant members to leave speaking space for
others. Making participation states visible can help members become more aware of inter-
action distribution within the group [3] and support the transition from external constraints
to internal self-regulation [56]. However, some members’silence did not stem from in-
sufficient motivation, but from a lack of expressible ideas or arguments. Nudging mainly
influences behavior by changing action resistance and attention allocation [57], rather than
directly generating new cognitive resources. This defines the boundary of behavioral cues:


                                             47
 they mainly address the distribution of participation opportunities, and when the collabo-
 ration bottleneck shifts toward content generation and idea deepening, the system needs to
 further turn toward cognitive support. Therefore, translating a participation reminder into
 actual participation requires the cue to be noticed, the member to be willing to respond, and
 that response to develop into a substantive contribution. A breakdown at any of these stages
 may result in the cue merely increasing opportunities to speak or eliciting a surface-level
 response, without meaningfully deepening the discussion.

     At the cognitive level, Study 2 revealed another boundary: cognitive cues can change
 discourse structure, but they do not necessarily ensure that structural changes translate into
 better task decisions. The results of EngageCue 2.0 showed that the proportion of Integration
 significantly increased and the proportion of Exploration decreased under the smart glasses
 condition, indicating that cues could push groups from merely exchanging ideas toward
 synthesis and integration. This is consistent with the theoretical claim that high-quality
 discussion needs to move beyond idea exploration toward integration and problem solving
 [51], and it also echoes recent research suggesting that the value of AI does not necessarily
 lie in directly providing answers, but in guiding higher-order thinking processes through
 questions and alternative perspectives [58]. However, the deepening of discourse structure is
 not equivalent to improvement in task outcomes. Group final performance also depends on
 whether the integrated content is accurate, whether members trust that content, and whether
 it truly enters the final decision. In other words, moving from a conceptual cue to a final
 conclusion requires the content to be discussed, verified, and substantively adopted. The
 increase in Integration therefore indicates a change in discourse structure, but does not by
 itself demonstrate that the integrated content was adequately verified or ultimately shaped
 the group’s final judgment.

     Taken together, the two studies reveal a design boundary that runs across both the behav-
 ioral and cognitive levels: AI cues can reshape participation structures and discourse forms
 in the collaboration process, but this reshaping primarily acts on the“form”rather than the
“content”of collaboration. It changes the distribution and organization of discussion, but
 does not directly generate new cognitive resources or ensure that relevant content is substan-



                                              48
tively adopted. Study 1 shows that even when members gain more opportunities to speak,
they may not necessarily offer substantive contributions. Study 2 similarly shows that even
when the discussion contains more integration of viewpoints, the resulting content may not
be adequately verified or incorporated into the final decision. Thus, in designing AI sup-
port for face-to-face collaboration, process regulation and outcome improvement should be
treated as goals at different levels: the former can create conditions for the latter, but cannot
guarantee that it will occur.



6.2     The Gap Between Process Improvement and Outcome
        Improvement

The pattern jointly revealed by the two studies—process improvement without changes in
outcomes—points to a deeper gap: AI cues can reshape the external structure of collabora-
tion, but the generation of outcomes depends on members’internal cognitive integration of
content. Between the two, a key intermediate link is missing: whether users truly internalize
cue content into their own thinking and decision-making. The existing one-way push-based
cue design has not yet effectively reached this link.

    At the behavioral level (Study 1), there is a cognitive gap between the balancing of
speaking opportunities and the deepening of discussion content. Behavioral engagement
and cognitive engagement are two independent dimensions, and improvement in the former
does not automatically lead to deepening in the latter [17]. Prior research further shows that
behavioral indicators such as speaking frequency are surface-level signals of participation:
frequent speaking does not equal cognitive depth, and silence does not equal disengagement
[59]. More importantly, when members lack cognitive content as support, speaking behav-
iors triggered by cues may remain at the level of surface compliance rather than substantive
contribution, making it difficult to truly advance the collaboration process [60]. The inter-
view data from Study 1 are highly consistent with this pattern: the root reason for some
members’silence was a lack of expressible ideas rather than insufficient motivation. This
shows that behavioral cues can create more opportunities for members to speak, but cannot
ensure that they have something substantive to contribute; therefore, receiving a participa-

                                               49
tion reminder does not necessarily amount to genuinely participating in the discussion.

   At the cognitive level (Study 2), there is an adoption-level gap between improvement
in discourse structure and improvement in decision quality. Whether cue content truly en-
ters the final decision depends on whether members are willing to invest cognitive effort
in verifying and adopting it. Research shows that when users are confident in their own
judgments, they tend to ignore AI suggestions, and human intuition directly affects the ex-
tent to which users rely on AI advice [61]. In AI-assisted decision-making contexts, users
need to continuously judge “whether to trust AI or trust themselves,”and this judgment
is constrained by their perceived credibility of AI [62]. In addition, whether AI suggestions
can improve decisions also depends on the costs and benefits of user participation in verifi-
cation: when verification costs are too high, suggestions will not be automatically adopted
[63]. The data from Study 2 support this mechanism: half of the groups in the experimental
condition did not incorporate cue content into their final ranking decisions, and group judg-
ments still mainly relied on their own discussion. Thus, moving from the introduction of
a cue to a final conclusion requires the content to be integrated, verified, and adopted. An
increase in Integration indicates a change in discourse structure, but if members do not trust
the cue, are unwilling to invest effort in verifying it, or ultimately continue to rely on their
own judgments, the integrated content may still not enter the final decision.

   Synthesizing the two levels, the root of the gap lies in the fact that the existing cue de-
sign primarily reaches the external form of collaboration and lacks mechanisms to support
users’active verification, reflection, and integration. Study 1 shows that more opportuni-
ties to speak do not necessarily lead to substantive contributions; Study 2 shows that greater
integration of viewpoints does not necessarily mean that the relevant content has been ver-
ified, adopted, and incorporated into the final decision. To bridge the gap between process
improvement and outcome improvement, future systems need to advance in two directions:
first, improving the degree to which cue content is anchored in the current discussion context
to reduce users’cognitive cost of verification; and second, shifting from one-way push to-
ward two-way interaction mechanisms that support users’active reflection and integration,
making structural improvements at the process level more likely to translate into substantive



                                              50
outcome improvements.



6.3     Design Implications for Cue Presentation: Timing, Tone,
        and Format

The two studies jointly show that the way cues are presented has a decisive influence on the
extent to which they are perceived and adopted. Timing, tone, and format are not merely
auxiliary design elements attached to content; rather, they constitute three key moderating
factors of cue effectiveness. The design quality at the presentation level directly determines
whether improvements at the content level can truly reach users.

   At the level of timing, the mismatch between cue timing and users’current cognitive
rhythm was a core issue that appeared in both studies. In Study 1, most participants per-
ceived the cues as disruptive, and cues were considered appropriately timed only when they
happened to appear during natural pauses in discussion. In Study 2, some groups similarly
reported that cues interrupted ongoing speech or thought flows, while others reported that
the main issue was that the cues appeared after the discussion had already moved forward,
making the content clearly delayed. The root of this problem lies in the lack of reliable real-
time external signals for internal cognitive activity in face-to-face collaboration: silence
may indicate deep thinking rather than lack of participation, and existing systems have dif-
ficulty distinguishing between the two. This is both an unresolved state-detection challenge
at the research level and a temporal alignment challenge that is difficult to overcome at the
engineering level. Prior research has shown that effective intervention needs to be precisely
aligned with users’current states; inappropriate timing not only weakens intervention ef-
fects but may also trigger resistance [64]. In face-to-face social scenarios, the presentation
of AR glasses notifications, even when they do not completely interrupt conversation, can
still draw users’attention away from the conversational flow and impose costs on cogni-
tive continuity [47]. Future systems may explore the integration of multimodal data such
as eye-tracking and physiological signals to enable real-time sensing of cognitive states and
thereby improve the precision of intervention timing [65].

   At the level of tone, encouraging wording does not necessarily convey a sense of sin-

                                              51
cerity. This issue has two layers. The first concerns the perception of tone itself: research
shows that when users recognize that emotional expression comes from AI, their perceived
sincerity of the content systematically decreases, along with their trust and willingness to
adopt it [66]; the choice of tone also directly affects users’judgments of cue content and
decisions [67]. A deeper issue is that users have already formed low expectations of AI’s
ability to express emotion before interacting with it, and this prior belief makes it difficult
for any tone design to overcome the perceived upper limit of sincerity. Recent research has
found that disclosing AI authorship systematically reduces users’perceptions of sincerity
and care in the content, especially in social and interpersonal contexts [68]. The interview
data from Study 1 are highly consistent with this mechanism: the same encouraging tone
triggered sharply different interpretations among participants. Some felt encouraged, while
others considered it mechanical or even somewhat sarcastic, and the latter explicitly at-
tributed this to their low expectations of AI’s expressive ability.

    At the level of format, Study 2 revealed two design issues. In terms of sentence form, in-
terrogative AI cues have been found in some contexts to improve users’logical discernment
accuracy [69], but the interview data from Study 2 showed the opposite pattern: participants
generally preferred declarative sentences, while interrogative cues instead made users more
confused. This suggests that the effect of sentence form is moderated by task context. In
collaborative decision-making scenarios that require rapid integration of arguments, declar-
ative cues are more easily incorporated directly into the argumentation framework. Another
more fundamental issue is the anchoring effect of AI content accuracy on trust. Research
shows that the quality of the first AI suggestion has a significant anchoring effect on subse-
quent trust development; early errors lead to a significant decline in trust and are difficult to
repair quickly through later correct performance [70]. The early performance of AI systems
shapes users’baseline trust, and negative first impressions have a persistent inhibitory ef-
fect on subsequent trust recovery [71]. The data from Study 2 support this mechanism: in
groups where the first cue was of poor quality, participants tended to systematically ignore
all subsequent cues rather than evaluate each cue based on its specific content.

    Taken together, the three dimensions of timing, tone, and format show that the presen-



                                               52
tation of cues is as important as the quality of their content [72]. Misaligned timing causes
content to be ignored, distorted tone triggers resistance, and insufficient format design and
credibility make trust difficult to establish. Therefore, future systems need to advance in
three directions. In terms of timing, they should explore combining multimodal physiolog-
ical signals to sense users’cognitive states in real time and identify more appropriate inter-
vention windows. In terms of tone, they should avoid overly anthropomorphic emotional
expressions and instead adopt concise, function-oriented wording to reduce users’doubts
about AI sincerity. In terms of format, they should emphasize the quality of early system
outputs to establish an initial trust baseline and choose sentence forms according to task
type, making cues easier for users to incorporate into argumentation and decision-making
processes.

         LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

This thesis focuses on small-scale, co-located three-person student groups in a controlled
laboratory environment, examining the intervention effects of AI support in short-term, 30-
minute face-to-face collaborative discussions. This setting facilitates experimental control,
but differs substantially from real-world collaborative scenarios. In actual work or learning
contexts, group collaboration usually involves more diverse member compositions, more
complex task types, and collaboration cycles that last for weeks or even months. Short-
term studies may also be affected by the novelty of using smart glasses for the first time
and cannot capture habituation or cue blindness during prolonged use; longitudinal stud-
ies could examine these effects through changes in cue response and adoption rates over
time. Moreover, findings from three-person groups cannot be directly generalized to larger
groups, where speaker identification, equitable cue allocation, and group coordination be-
come more complex. All participants were recruited from a single university community and
were relatively homogeneous in terms of technology acceptance and collaboration habits.
Therefore, the transferability of the findings to broader contexts, such as workplace teams
and cross-cultural collaborative groups, remains to be validated. Future research should con-
duct longer-term longitudinal deployments to examine the sustainability of system effects in
real course groups or work teams, and extend the task types to open-ended collaborative sce-
narios such as design reviews and strategy discussions, thereby examining the boundaries
within which the findings of this thesis apply.

   The data collected by the two systems developed in this thesis remain largely limited to
observable behavioral and discourse features. EngageCue 1.0 combined speech, editing, and
browsing behaviors to analyze participation distribution, whereas EngageCue 2.0 focused
more specifically on speech and incorporated content-related features such as argumentation
density, semantic repetition, and reasoning and evidence status. Although these indicators


                                             54
can reflect members’ participation behaviors and the surface structure of discourse, they
cannot directly capture members’ internal cognitive states or determine whether a group had
achieved genuine knowledge co-construction and idea integration. Future research could
introduce richer multimodal signals, such as eye tracking and physiological signals, and
combine them with analytical frameworks from knowledge-construction research to develop
deeper quantitative measures of discussion quality. This would provide a more reliable
sensing foundation for matching intervention timing and content more precisely.

   Both systems also relied on fixed parameters configured for the current prototypes and
experimental contexts. In Study 1, EngageCue 1.0 used fixed speech-segment lengths, log-
ging and analysis windows, and predefined weights to integrate speech, editing, and brows-
ing behaviors into different participation levels. In Study 2, EngageCue 2.0 further used
fixed thresholds for indicators including speaking proportion, silence duration, lexical rich-
ness, semantic repetition, and argumentation density. These thresholds were calibrated by
replaying Study 1 discussion data offline and were combined with multiple signals, includ-
ing reasoning, evidence, and discussion content, to reduce false triggers caused by any single
indicator. However, this thesis did not systematically examine how alternative parameter
settings would affect state classification, cue frequency, or intervention decisions in either
system, nor did it validate their adaptability across tasks, speaking styles, group sizes, or
participant populations. Future research should evaluate the robustness of these parameters
using larger and more diverse datasets and explore sensing and intervention mechanisms
that can adapt dynamically to task characteristics, group states, and individual participation
patterns.

   In addition, the use of AI systems in face-to-face collaboration involves potential ethical
and social implications that should be taken seriously in future research. While private
HUD cues improve individual opportunities for participation, they also create information
asymmetry among group members: members who receive cues are externally influenced
during the discussion, while other members are unaware of this influence. This may create
implicit interference with the authenticity and fairness of group interaction. In addition, the
system’s continuous monitoring and quantification of members’ participation states involve
the collection and AI interpretation of personal behavioral data, raising concerns that require

                                              55
careful consideration in terms of user autonomy and privacy protection. Future research
should introduce more transparent consent and disclosure mechanisms into system design
and explore how to achieve a more reasonable balance between assistive effects and user
autonomy.

                                  CHAPTER 8

                                CONCLUSION

Through a formative study and two iterations of system development, this thesis designed
and evaluated the EngageCue system along a trajectory from behavioral engagement to cog-
nitive engagement. The results of the two user studies showed that smart-glasses-based AI
support significantly improved the balance of speaking distribution and the proportion of
higher-order cognitive engagement, but these structural improvements at the process level
did not consistently translate into improvements in task outcomes. Cues can reshape the
distribution and rhythm of discussion, but they cannot replace members’own cognitive
generation, nor can they ensure that cue content is truly internalized as a basis for decision-
making. The two studies also show that the presentation of cues is as important as the
content itself. Intervention timing should be aligned with users’cognitive rhythm rather
than interrupting the flow of thought; tone should be concise and function-oriented rather
than overly anthropomorphic, so as to avoid weakening perceived sincerity; sentence form
should preferably be declarative so that users can directly incorporate cues into their argu-
mentation framework; and the content quality of the first cue has an anchoring effect on the
establishment of subsequent trust. These findings provide concrete empirical evidence for
the design of AI systems for face-to-face collaboration and point to directions for bridging
the gap between process improvement and outcome improvement.

A.7 Study 2 AI Analysis Layer Implementation

Appendix A.7 presents the prompt design used in EngageCue 2.0 for cognitive engagement
analysis and cue generation. The prompt pipeline consisted of three main components:
perception analysis, action decision, and cue generation. These prompts enabled the system
to analyze real-time group discussion transcripts, identify members’ cognitive engagement
states, determine whether an intervention was needed, and generate short cues suitable for
presentation on the smart-glasses HUD.

     The system input included the most recent discussion transcript, a short discussion sum-
mary, and member-level indicators, such as speaking ratio, silence duration, vocabulary
richness, semantic repetition, argumentation density, reasoning status, and evidence status.
Recent utterances were also preserved as contextual anchors so that the generated cues could
refer to concrete discussion content rather than abstract participation labels.

     Because EngageCue 2.0 operated in Chinese during the study, the example utterances,
model rationales, generated cues, and prompt templates below are presented as English
translations for readability. The original field names, thresholds, classification logic, and
output constraints were preserved.

Prompt 2: Action Decision Prompt


This prompt was used to determine whether an intervention should be triggered for each
member and, if so, which type of cognitive challenge should be addressed. The system
distinguished between three output categories: stagnation, shallow, and none. Stagnation
referred to low participation or difficulty re-entering the discussion, while shallow referred
to contributions that had sufficient verbal presence but lacked reasoning, evidence, or elab-
oration. When evidence was insufficient, the default output was none, so as to avoid unnec-
essary intervention.

stagnation indicates insufficient participation or idea stagnation.
Strong signal, sufficient by itself:
- speaking_ratio < 0.15, indicating very little speech and serving as the most
    direct signal of insufficient participation.
Weak signals, insufficient by themselves:
- info_gain < 0.3, indicating little new content but possibly reflecting attentive

- info_gain < 0.3 and speaking_ratio < 0.25.

Prompt 3: Cue Generation Prompt


For individual interventions, cue generation was embedded in the action decision prompt.
The generated cue was required to be short, concrete, and anchored to a specific utterance or
unresolved issue in the ongoing discussion. The prompt explicitly prohibited generic par-
ticipation reminders, evaluative language, and direct comments on members’ participation
levels. This design aimed to make the cue feel like a lightweight conversational follow-up
rather than an external evaluation.
[Constraints for user-visible content]
analysis is internal and may explain the trigger. content is shown to the user and
     must never reveal internal judgments, indicators, or participation status.
content must identify a specific claim, undeveloped direction, or implicit
    disagreement in the transcript and frame it as a question. Do not generate a
    question from a topic label alone.
For shallow, content must probe one level deeper into the anchored utterance by
    identifying an unstated assumption, implicit position, or missing reason. Do
    not generate a generic question detached from the anchor.
For stagnation, content must raise a specific contested point from the current
    discussion so that the member can directly agree, disagree, or add information,
     rather than merely inviting the member to speak.
Use a friendly and lightweight tone, as if a peer were naturally following up. The
     question itself must contain a position, claim, or dilemma that gives the

    member something concrete to address.
content must not include generic or evaluative phrases such as: speaking too
    little, low participation, we have not heard your voice, we look forward to
    hearing from you, everyone expects, please share more, say more, add your view,
     the meeting is in progress, the discussion is lively, content quality is
    normal, or no guidance is needed.
content must not contain dash characters. Do not connect clauses with a dash.
content must not be a purely invitational question with no specific position or
    claim.
Do not address the user by name, evaluate the user's performance, lecture the user
    , or call on the user like a moderator.
If no specific content in the transcript can support a follow-up, leave content
    empty and set needs_prompt=false.
Examples of content anchored in specific claims rather than topic labels:
- Is humor relieving pressure or avoiding it?
- If people do not want to be constantly available, does the partner relationship
    still hold?
- Could turning pressure into humor obscure the real problem?
- If efficiency is high but no one recognizes it, is it still success?

Filtering Rules


Generated cues were not pushed directly to participants. Before presentation, cues were
processed through deduplication, semantic similarity filtering, VAD-based speaking detec-
tion, and cooldown control. These filtering rules were used to avoid repeated or mistimed
prompts, especially prompts that might appear while a participant was speaking or while
the group was already actively discussing. Through this process, EngageCue 2.0 aimed to
provide lightweight cognitive support while minimizing interruption to the natural rhythm
